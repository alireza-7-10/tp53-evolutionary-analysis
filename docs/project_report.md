# Tracing the Pattern of Positive Selection and Structural Conservation in the Genome Guardian Gene (TP53) during Vertebrate Evolution

**Author:** Alireza Safari  
**Date:** August 2026

## Abstract

The tumor suppressor gene TP53 plays a fundamental role in maintaining genome stability, controlling the cell cycle, and inducing programmed cell death. To investigate the evolutionary patterns of this gene in vertebrates, complete coding sequences (CDS) of the TP53 gene were examined in seven species, including human (Homo sapiens), rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta), house mouse (Mus musculus), Norway rat (Rattus norvegicus), cattle (Bos taurus), dog (Canis lupus familiaris), and zebrafish (Danio rerio). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the MUSCLE algorithm, and a phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method based on the Tamura–Nei model with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA 12 software. In addition, patterns of natural selection at the codon level were assessed using the Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL) method on the Datamonkey server, with a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05. The results of genetic distance analysis showed that the lowest divergence was observed between human and rhesus macaque, with a genetic distance of 0.0366 (3.66%), whereas the greatest divergence belonged to zebrafish compared with the mammalian species, ranging from 52.70% to 57.62%. The phylogenetic tree also revealed the close relationship between the TP53 sequences of human and rhesus macaque, as well as between house mouse and Norway rat, with bootstrap values of 99% and 100%, respectively. However, some internal nodes exhibited lower bootstrap support, indicating relative uncertainty in certain phylogenetic relationships. FEL analysis identified, among 389 non-invariant sites, 12 codons under purifying selection and 4 codons under diversifying/positive selection at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. These findings indicate that, alongside considerable conservation of the TP53 sequence among certain closely related species, a limited number of codon sites have experienced different patterns of selective pressure throughout evolution. Overall, the results indicate substantial evolutionary conservation of TP53, particularly among closely related mammals, along with the presence of a limited number of sites under purifying and diversifying selection — a pattern consistent with the functional importance of this gene and the necessity of preserving certain molecular features throughout its evolution.

## Introduction

Cancer is one of the most complex biological disorders, arising and progressing as a result of disruption in fundamental cellular processes, including cell cycle control, DNA damage repair, differentiation, and cell death. At the center of the molecular networks responsible for maintaining genomic stability lies the tumor suppressor gene TP53, which encodes the p53 protein. Widely known as the "Guardian of the Genome," this protein is a key transcription factor that becomes activated in response to a broad range of cellular stresses, including DNA damage, hypoxia, and oncogene activation [1,2]. Activation of p53 can prevent the proliferation and survival of genetically damaged cells through the induction of cell cycle arrest—thereby allowing time for DNA repair—induction of cellular senescence, or activation of apoptotic pathways [2]. Consequently, disruption of this pathway, whether due to mutations in TP53 or dysregulation of its regulators, is considered one of the key events associated with the initiation and progression of a wide range of human cancers [2].
Structurally, p53 is a multi-domain transcription factor that functions as a tetramer and consists of several functional regions. These include the transactivation domain (TAD) at the N-terminus, the proline-rich domain (PRD), the central DNA-binding domain (DBD), the oligomerization domain (OD), and the C-terminal regulatory domain (CTD). Among these regions, the DBD plays a critical role in recognizing target DNA sequences and regulating the expression of p53-dependent genes, and a substantial proportion of oncogenic TP53 mutations have been reported within this domain [2,3]. The functional conservation of p53 across vertebrates reflects the evolutionary importance of this protein in maintaining cellular homeostasis and genomic stability; however, species-specific differences in traits such as body size, lifespan, metabolic rate, and physiological conditions may be associated with variations in the evolutionary mechanisms underlying cancer control [3]. In this context, "Peto's Paradox" refers to the observation that increased body size or lifespan in certain species is not necessarily accompanied by a proportional increase in cancer risk—a phenomenon that highlights the importance of investigating the evolutionary mechanisms related to cancer control [10].
Evolutionary and in silico bioinformatic studies enable the investigation of patterns of change, conservation, and divergence of genes among different species. Within this framework, sequence comparison and calculation of genetic distances can reveal the degree of similarity and divergence between species, while phylogenetic tree reconstruction allows examination of the evolutionary relationships among the sequences under study. Furthermore, analysis of natural selection pressure at the codon level can identify sites that have been subject to different constraints throughout evolution. Purifying (negative) selection is typically associated with the elimination of deleterious amino acid changes and the preservation of sites important for protein structure or function, whereas positive (diversifying) selection may be associated with an increased frequency of beneficial amino acid changes within populations. In this regard, the Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL) method, by comparing nonsynonymous and synonymous substitution rates at the codon level, enables the identification of sites showing statistical evidence of purifying or positive selection [7,8].
Despite extensive research on the function of p53 and its role in cancer, a simultaneous examination of sequence conservation patterns, phylogenetic relationships, and natural selection pressure on TP53 across different vertebrate species can provide a broader evolutionary perspective on this gene. In particular, identifying codon sites with evidence of purifying or positive selection can be useful in pinpointing regions of the protein that are likely subject to distinct functional constraints. Therefore, the aim of the present study is to conduct a comparative in silico investigation—assessing genetic distance, reconstructing phylogenetic relationships, and analyzing natural selection pressure—on the complete coding sequence (CDS) of the TP53 gene in seven representative vertebrate species: human (Homo sapiens), rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta), house mouse (Mus musculus), Norway rat (Rattus norvegicus), cattle (Bos taurus), dog (Canis lupus familiaris), and zebrafish (Danio rerio). In this study, CDS sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm, and phylogenetic relationships were reconstructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method based on the Tamura–Nei model. In addition, selection pressure at the codon level was evaluated using the FEL method on the Datamonkey server [6–8]. This approach allows for the simultaneous assessment of the degree of conservation and evolutionary divergence of TP53, as well as the identification of sites showing statistical evidence of differential selective pressure, thereby contributing to a better understanding of the relationship between the functional conservation of this gene and its evolutionary changes across vertebrates.
