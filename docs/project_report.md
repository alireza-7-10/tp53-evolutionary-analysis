# Tracing the Pattern of Positive Selection and Structural Conservation in the Genome Guardian Gene (TP53) during Vertebrate Evolution

**Author:** Alireza Safari  
**Date:** August 2026

## Abstract

The tumor suppressor gene TP53 plays a fundamental role in maintaining genome stability, controlling the cell cycle, and inducing programmed cell death. To investigate the evolutionary patterns of this gene in vertebrates, complete coding sequences (CDS) of the TP53 gene were examined in seven species, including human (Homo sapiens), rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta), house mouse (Mus musculus), Norway rat (Rattus norvegicus), cattle (Bos taurus), dog (Canis lupus familiaris), and zebrafish (Danio rerio). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the MUSCLE algorithm, and a phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method based on the Tamura–Nei model with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA 12 software. In addition, patterns of natural selection at the codon level were assessed using the Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL) method on the Datamonkey server, with a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05. The results of genetic distance analysis showed that the lowest divergence was observed between human and rhesus macaque, with a genetic distance of 0.0366 (3.66%), whereas the greatest divergence belonged to zebrafish compared with the mammalian species, ranging from 52.70% to 57.62%. The phylogenetic tree also revealed the close relationship between the TP53 sequences of human and rhesus macaque, as well as between house mouse and Norway rat, with bootstrap values of 99% and 100%, respectively. However, some internal nodes exhibited lower bootstrap support, indicating relative uncertainty in certain phylogenetic relationships. FEL analysis identified, among 389 non-invariant sites, 12 codons under purifying selection and 4 codons under diversifying/positive selection at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. These findings indicate that, alongside considerable conservation of the TP53 sequence among certain closely related species, a limited number of codon sites have experienced different patterns of selective pressure throughout evolution. Overall, the results indicate substantial evolutionary conservation of TP53, particularly among closely related mammals, along with the presence of a limited number of sites under purifying and diversifying selection — a pattern consistent with the functional importance of this gene and the necessity of preserving certain molecular features throughout its evolution.

## Introduction

Cancer is one of the most complex biological disorders, arising and progressing as a result of disruption in fundamental cellular processes, including cell cycle control, DNA damage repair, differentiation, and cell death. At the center of the molecular networks responsible for maintaining genomic stability lies the tumor suppressor gene TP53, which encodes the p53 protein. Widely known as the "Guardian of the Genome," this protein is a key transcription factor that becomes activated in response to a broad range of cellular stresses, including DNA damage, hypoxia, and oncogene activation [1,2]. Activation of p53 can prevent the proliferation and survival of genetically damaged cells through the induction of cell cycle arrest—thereby allowing time for DNA repair—induction of cellular senescence, or activation of apoptotic pathways [2]. Consequently, disruption of this pathway, whether due to mutations in TP53 or dysregulation of its regulators, is considered one of the key events associated with the initiation and progression of a wide range of human cancers [2].
Structurally, p53 is a multi-domain transcription factor that functions as a tetramer and consists of several functional regions. These include the transactivation domain (TAD) at the N-terminus, the proline-rich domain (PRD), the central DNA-binding domain (DBD), the oligomerization domain (OD), and the C-terminal regulatory domain (CTD). Among these regions, the DBD plays a critical role in recognizing target DNA sequences and regulating the expression of p53-dependent genes, and a substantial proportion of oncogenic TP53 mutations have been reported within this domain [2,3]. The functional conservation of p53 across vertebrates reflects the evolutionary importance of this protein in maintaining cellular homeostasis and genomic stability; however, species-specific differences in traits such as body size, lifespan, metabolic rate, and physiological conditions may be associated with variations in the evolutionary mechanisms underlying cancer control [3]. In this context, "Peto's Paradox" refers to the observation that increased body size or lifespan in certain species is not necessarily accompanied by a proportional increase in cancer risk—a phenomenon that highlights the importance of investigating the evolutionary mechanisms related to cancer control [10].
Evolutionary and in silico bioinformatic studies enable the investigation of patterns of change, conservation, and divergence of genes among different species. Within this framework, sequence comparison and calculation of genetic distances can reveal the degree of similarity and divergence between species, while phylogenetic tree reconstruction allows examination of the evolutionary relationships among the sequences under study. Furthermore, analysis of natural selection pressure at the codon level can identify sites that have been subject to different constraints throughout evolution. Purifying (negative) selection is typically associated with the elimination of deleterious amino acid changes and the preservation of sites important for protein structure or function, whereas positive (diversifying) selection may be associated with an increased frequency of beneficial amino acid changes within populations. In this regard, the Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL) method, by comparing nonsynonymous and synonymous substitution rates at the codon level, enables the identification of sites showing statistical evidence of purifying or positive selection [7,8].
Despite extensive research on the function of p53 and its role in cancer, a simultaneous examination of sequence conservation patterns, phylogenetic relationships, and natural selection pressure on TP53 across different vertebrate species can provide a broader evolutionary perspective on this gene. In particular, identifying codon sites with evidence of purifying or positive selection can be useful in pinpointing regions of the protein that are likely subject to distinct functional constraints. Therefore, the aim of the present study is to conduct a comparative in silico investigation—assessing genetic distance, reconstructing phylogenetic relationships, and analyzing natural selection pressure—on the complete coding sequence (CDS) of the TP53 gene in seven representative vertebrate species: human (Homo sapiens), rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta), house mouse (Mus musculus), Norway rat (Rattus norvegicus), cattle (Bos taurus), dog (Canis lupus familiaris), and zebrafish (Danio rerio). In this study, CDS sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm, and phylogenetic relationships were reconstructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method based on the Tamura–Nei model. In addition, selection pressure at the codon level was evaluated using the FEL method on the Datamonkey server [6–8]. This approach allows for the simultaneous assessment of the degree of conservation and evolutionary divergence of TP53, as well as the identification of sites showing statistical evidence of differential selective pressure, thereby contributing to a better understanding of the relationship between the functional conservation of this gene and its evolutionary changes across vertebrates.

## Materials and Methods

### 1. Data Retrieval
The complete coding sequences (CDS) of the TP53 gene for seven species—human, rhesus macaque, house mouse, Norway rat, cattle, dog, and zebrafish—were retrieved from the NCBI database. For each sequence, the corresponding accession number was recorded, and the sequences were checked for CDS completeness, correct reading frame, and suitability for evolutionary comparisons. Danio rerio was considered as the outgroup in the phylogenetic analysis.

### 2. Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA)
Multiple sequence alignment of the CDS sequences was performed using MEGA 12 software and the MUSCLE algorithm [5,6]. The purpose of the alignment was to create a common framework for comparing nucleotide and codon positions among the species under study. Prior to conducting the evolutionary analyses, the stop codon was removed from the end of the CDS sequences, and the reading frame of the sequences was checked to ensure the correct coding frame was maintained and to avoid errors resulting from frameshifts.

### 3. Genetic Distance Estimation
To assess the degree of nucleotide divergence among the species under study, pairwise genetic distances between the CDS sequences were calculated. These calculations were based on the Tamura–Nei (TN93) substitution model [4]. All genetic distance calculations were performed in MEGA 12 software, and the resulting matrix was used to evaluate the degree of similarity and evolutionary divergence among the species [5].

### 4. Phylogenetic Tree Reconstruction
The phylogenetic tree of the species under study was reconstructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method based on the Tamura–Nei (TN93) substitution model in MEGA 12 software [4,5]. To assess the statistical support of the tree nodes, a bootstrap test with 1000 replicates was performed. Bootstrap values were considered as an indicator of the degree of data support for the observed phylogenetic relationships. Danio rerio was also used as the outgroup to determine the relative position of the mammalian group and to root the tree.

### 5. Selection Pressure Analysis
To investigate natural selection pressure at the codon level, the alignment of the CDS sequences, together with the phylogenetic tree obtained from the Maximum Likelihood analysis, was used to perform Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL) analysis on the Datamonkey server [7,8]. The FEL method, using a maximum likelihood framework, estimates the synonymous (α) and nonsynonymous (β) substitution rates at each codon position and, based on the comparison of these two rates, allows for the identification of sites showing statistical evidence of purifying or diversifying selection [7].
In this study, p ≤ 0.05 was considered the criterion for statistical significance in identifying sites under selection. Within the FEL framework, sites where the nonsynonymous substitution rate was significantly higher than the synonymous substitution rate (β > α) were classified as sites under diversifying selection, while sites where the synonymous substitution rate was higher than the nonsynonymous substitution rate (α > β) were classified as sites under purifying selection [7].
In the final analysis, 393 codons in the alignment were examined, of which 4 codons were invariable and 389 non-invariant sites were considered for the evaluation of selection pressure. The results of the FEL analysis and the number of sites showing statistically significant evidence of purifying and diversifying selection are reported in the Results section.

## Results

### 1. Pairwise Genetic Distance

Calculation of pairwise genetic distances based on the Tamura–Nei model in MEGA 12 software revealed the extent of nucleotide divergence among the CDS sequences of the TP53 gene in the seven species examined (Table 2).
The lowest genetic distance was observed between human (Homo sapiens) and rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta), with a value of 0.0366, indicating the greatest similarity of the TP53 sequence among the species studied. Among rodents, the genetic distance between house mouse (Mus musculus) and Norway rat (Rattus norvegicus) was found to be 0.0999.
The genetic distances between human and Mus musculus and Rattus norvegicus were 0.2286 and 0.2116, respectively. Likewise, the genetic distances between human and cattle (Bos taurus) and dog (Canis lupus familiaris) were calculated as 0.1798 and 0.1794, respectively.
In contrast, Danio rerio showed the greatest genetic distance relative to the other species studied. Its distance from the mammalian species ranged from 0.5270 to 0.5762. The largest distance was between Danio rerio and Mus musculus (0.5762), and the smallest was between Danio rerio and Canis lupus familiaris (0.5270).

### 2. Phylogenetic Tree Reconstruction

Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method and the Tamura–Nei substitution model in MEGA 12 software. To evaluate the statistical support of the tree branches, a bootstrap test with 1000 replicates was used.
The resulting tree showed a clear separation of Danio rerio from the mammalian species (Figure 1). Among mammals, human (Homo sapiens) and rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta) formed a single clade, and this relationship was supported by a bootstrap value of 99%. Similarly, Mus musculus and Rattus norvegicus formed a separate clade, supported by a bootstrap value of 100%.
The main node representing the relationship between the primate clade and the rodent clade had a bootstrap value of 75%. Bos taurus branched adjacent to this group, with the corresponding node showing a bootstrap value of 52%. Canis lupus familiaris occupied a separate branch among the mammals.
Finally, Danio rerio occupied the basal position of the tree and was used as the outgroup. This pattern was consistent with the greater genetic distance of this species relative to the mammalian species, as observed in the previous section.

### 3. Assessment of Natural Selection Pressure Using the FEL Method

To examine the pattern of natural selection at the codon level, Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL) analysis was performed on the alignment of the TP53 gene CDS using the Datamonkey server. The dataset comprised 7 sequences, 393 codon sites, and one partition. Of the 393 sites, 389 non-invariant sites were evaluated for the selection test. The analysis output also reported an average of 10 branches used for testing per partition.
Using a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05, a total of 16 codon sites showed statistically significant evidence of natural selection. Of these, 4 sites were under positive/diversifying selection, and 12 sites were under purifying selection. Thus, 16 out of the 389 tested non-invariant codon sites—approximately 4.1% of the testable sites—showed significant evidence of selection. The Datamonkey output likewise directly reported 4 sites under positive selection and 12 sites under purifying selection at the p ≤ 0.05 level.
In contrast, 373 of the 389 non-invariant sites did not show sufficient statistical evidence to be classified as under positive or purifying selection and were placed in the neutral group. The remaining four sites in the alignment were invariant and were therefore excluded from the selection test.

### 3.1. Sites Under Purifying Selection

Among the significant sites, 12 codons were identified as being under purifying selection. These sites included codons 3, 22, 46, 64, 75, 142, 184, 193, 194, 252, 370, and 375.
At these sites, the synonymous substitution rate (α) was significantly higher than the nonsynonymous substitution rate (β), consistent with a pattern of purifying selection. The p-values for these sites ranged from 0.0155 to 0.0477.
Among the purifying sites, the lowest p-value corresponded to codon 75 (p = 0.0155), followed by codons 194, 252, and 193, with p-values of 0.0173, 0.0205, and 0.0235, respectively.

### 3.2. Sites Under Positive Selection

A total of 4 codons—14, 174, 318, and 380—showed significant evidence of positive/diversifying selection.
At these sites, the nonsynonymous substitution rate (β) was estimated to be higher than the synonymous substitution rate (α), and the statistical test also showed a significant difference between the two rates. The p-values for these four sites were 0.0462, 0.0443, 0.0372, and 0.0185, respectively.
Among these sites, codon 380 showed the lowest p-value (0.0185) and thus the strongest statistical evidence of positive selection among the identified sites.

## Discussion

TP53 is one of the most important tumor suppressor genes in vertebrates, and the p53 protein plays a fundamental role in maintaining genomic stability, controlling the cell cycle, responding to DNA damage, and inducing cell death [1,2]. Given the functional importance of this protein, a substantial portion of the TP53 sequence would be expected to be under selective constraint throughout evolution. In the present study, comparative and phylogenetic approaches were used to examine the pattern of sequence divergence, evolutionary relationships, and natural selection pressure in the CDS of the TP53 gene across seven vertebrate species.

### Genetic Divergence and Evolutionary Conservation

The genetic distance results showed that the lowest degree of divergence was observed between human (Homo sapiens) and rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta), with a calculated distance of 0.0366, corresponding to 3.66%. This value reflects the high similarity of the TP53 sequence between the two primate species. Likewise, the genetic distance between house mouse (Mus musculus) and Norway rat (Rattus norvegicus) was 9.99%, consistent with the closer evolutionary relationship between these two species compared to the other pairs examined.
In contrast, Danio rerio showed the greatest genetic distance from the mammalian species, with distances between zebrafish and mammals ranging from approximately 52.7% to 57.6%. This pattern can be interpreted within the context of the greater evolutionary distance between bony fish and mammals. Therefore, although genetic distance values merely reflect the degree of sequence divergence and should not, on their own, be taken as a direct measure of "functional conservation," the overall pattern observed was consistent with the expected evolutionary relationships among the groups studied.

### Phylogenetic Relationships and Statistical Node Support

The Maximum Likelihood phylogenetic tree based on the Tamura–Nei model showed the expected relative separation of evolutionary groups. In this tree, human and rhesus macaque formed a clade with 99% bootstrap support, while house mouse and Norway rat formed a branch with 100% support. These high bootstrap values indicate considerable stability of these two relationships within the dataset examined.
However, not all tree nodes had equal statistical support. In particular, some internal nodes had lower bootstrap values, including approximately 75% and 52%. Therefore, it cannot be claimed, based on these data, that the entire tree topology reproduces the standard classification with complete certainty. The lower support at these nodes may reflect uncertainty in resolving certain intermediate branching points, potentially influenced by the limited number of species, the use of a single gene, and the amount of phylogenetic information contained within the TP53 sequence. Accordingly, relationships with low bootstrap support should be interpreted with greater caution, and confirming them would require the use of additional genes and a greater number of species.
The placement of Danio rerio on the outer branch, given its evolutionary distance from mammals, supports the use of this species as an outgroup within the framework of the present analysis. However, this result does not imply a complete reconstruction of vertebrate evolutionary history, but only reflects the relationships among the sequences examined.

### Pattern of Natural Selection at the Codon Level

Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL) analysis showed that, of the 393 codons in the alignment, 4 sites were invariant and 389 sites were non-invariant and were evaluated in the selection analysis. Using a significance criterion of p ≤ 0.05, a total of 16 sites showed statistical evidence of selection, comprising 12 sites under purifying selection and 4 sites under positive/diversifying selection.
The greater number of sites under purifying selection compared to sites under positive selection is consistent with the biological expectation for a gene with a fundamental role in regulating cellular processes. Purifying selection typically eliminates deleterious nonsynonymous changes and can therefore help preserve the functional properties of the protein throughout evolution [7]. However, the presence of 12 sites under purifying selection in the present analysis should not, on its own, be taken as direct proof of the functional importance of each of these sites, since FEL is a statistical method for identifying patterns of difference between synonymous and nonsynonymous substitution rates at the codon level.
Among the four sites with evidence of positive selection, codons 14, 174, 318, and 380 were identified. The lowest p-value among all 16 significant sites belonged to codon 75 (p = 0.0155); among the 4 sites under positive selection, codon 380 (p = 0.0185) had the lowest value. These findings may indicate heterogeneity in selective pressures along the TP53 sequence; however, the identification of a site as being under positive selection by FEL alone does not, by itself, prove phenotypic adaptation or a direct role for that site in the adaptation of a particular lineage.

### Selected Sites within the DNA-Binding Domain

Structurally, the DNA-binding domain (DBD) of the p53 protein spans approximately amino acids 102 to 292, and a major part of the regulatory function of p53 depends on this region [2,3]. In the FEL results of the present study, several sites under purifying selection were identified within this region, including codons 142, 184, 193, 194, and 252. The presence of these sites within the DBD is consistent with the high functional importance of this region and the necessity of preserving its structure and function.
In contrast, among the four sites with evidence of positive selection, only codon 174 fell within the DBD region. Thus, contrary to an interpretation that might emphasize a concentration of positive selection within the DBD, the present results do not show such a pattern; rather, evidence of positive selection is distributed across different parts of the protein. This highlights the importance of interpreting selected sites within the context of the protein's overall architecture.

### Significance of the N- and C-Terminal Regions

Some significant sites were located outside the DBD. Codons 14, 22, 46, 64, and 75 are located in the N-terminal region of the protein, while codons 370, 375, and 380 are located in the C-terminal region. Among these sites, codon 380 showed evidence of positive selection, whereas codons 370 and 375 were under purifying selection. This pattern indicates that selective pressure in the C-terminal region is also not uniform, and different sites within this region may be subject to different evolutionary constraints.
On the other hand, the N-terminal region of p53 is important in regulating transcriptional activity and interacting with various regulators. One of the most important negative regulators of p53 is the MDM2 protein, which, by binding to the N-terminal region of p53, inhibits its transcriptional activity and, through its ubiquitin ligase activity, contributes to regulating the stability and degradation of p53. Additionally, p53 and MDM2 regulate each other through a negative feedback loop [11]. Therefore, the conservation or changes observed in the N-terminal regulatory regions can be considered within the framework of the role of these regions in the p53 regulatory network; although the present results alone do not establish a direct link between the identified sites and the p53–MDM2 interaction.

### Evolutionary Interpretation of Positively Selected Sites

The presence of four sites with statistical evidence of positive selection, compared to the larger number of sites under purifying selection, suggests that alongside the extensive conservation of the TP53 sequence, certain positions may exhibit greater flexibility throughout evolution. However, this finding should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of lineage-specific positive selection or a particular evolutionary adaptation.
The reason for this caution is that FEL is fundamentally designed to detect site-level selection under a constant pattern across the phylogenetic tree, and for detecting episodic or lineage-specific positive selection, other specialized methods such as MEME or aBSREL are more appropriate [7,8]. Therefore, sites 14, 174, 318, and 380 should be regarded as candidates with statistical evidence of positive selection that warrant further investigation, rather than as proven adaptive sites.

### Study Limitations

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, only seven species and a single gene were examined; therefore, the results do not necessarily represent the full evolutionary diversity of TP53 in vertebrates. Second, the use of a single representative sequence per species limits the ability to assess intraspecific variation. Third, some phylogenetic tree nodes had moderate or low bootstrap values, indicating uncertainty in certain internal relationships. Fourth, FEL is a site-based analysis and is not, on its own, sufficient to examine episodic or lineage-specific selection. In addition, the use of a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05 without applying multiple-testing correction in this analysis may increase the likelihood of detecting some false-positive results; therefore, findings of positive selection should be validated with complementary and independent analyses.
In future studies, increasing the number of species, particularly through balanced sampling across different vertebrate lineages, could enhance the power of phylogenetic and natural selection analyses. In addition, using complementary methods such as MEME to detect episodic selection, as well as Bayesian or structural approaches, could be useful for a more precise evaluation of candidate sites. Mapping selected sites onto the three-dimensional structure of p53 could also help investigate the possible relationship between evolutionary changes and the structural features of the protein.

### Summary of the Discussion

Overall, the results of this study showed that TP53 exhibits a considerable degree of evolutionary conservation among the species examined, while the extent of divergence varies among different lineages. The lowest genetic distance, between human and rhesus macaque, was 3.66%, and the greatest distances were observed between zebrafish and mammals. The Maximum Likelihood tree also showed closer relationships among certain mammalian species with very high support, including 99% for human–rhesus macaque and 100% for mouse–rat; however, lower bootstrap values at some internal nodes indicate that not all relationships in the tree can be interpreted with the same level of confidence.
FEL analysis identified a total of 12 sites under purifying selection and 4 sites under positive selection among the 389 non-invariant sites. The predominance of purifying sites is consistent with the high functional importance of TP53, while the distribution of positively selected sites across different parts of the protein indicates heterogeneity in selective pressure along the sequence. The identified sites, particularly those located within the DBD, may be considered suitable targets for future structural and evolutionary investigations. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted as statistical, hypothesis-generating evidence, and additional data and complementary methods are needed to determine the precise functional role and pattern of selection across different lineages.

## Conclusion

In this study, the evolutionary pattern of the tumor suppressor gene TP53 was examined in seven vertebrate species using bioinformatic and phylogenetic approaches. Comparison of the complete coding sequences (CDS) showed that the degree of TP53 divergence varies among different species, with the lowest genetic distance observed between human (Homo sapiens) and rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta). Similarly, the relative closeness of house mouse (Mus musculus) and Norway rat (Rattus norvegicus) compared to the other species reflected greater conservation of the TP53 sequence among more closely related evolutionary groups.
Reconstruction of the phylogenetic tree using the Maximum Likelihood method and the Tamura–Nei model largely reflected the expected evolutionary relationships among the species. The formation of the primate clade with 99% bootstrap support and the rodent clade with 100% support indicated a high degree of stability for these relationships within the dataset studied. In contrast, Danio rerio occupied a separate and more basal branch relative to the mammals, consistent with its use as an outgroup in the phylogenetic analysis.
Natural selection analysis using the FEL method showed that the majority of the codon sites examined did not have sufficient statistical evidence to be classified as under positive or purifying selection; however, among the 389 non-invariant sites, 12 sites under purifying selection and 4 sites under positive/diversifying selection were identified at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. The predominance of sites under purifying selection over sites under positive selection is consistent with the importance of preserving the essential molecular functions of the p53 protein and the evolutionary constraints imposed on certain parts of this gene.
Overall, the findings of this study indicate that, while TP53 retains a considerable portion of its evolutionary features among vertebrates, certain codon sites have also undergone notable evolutionary changes. The combination of genetic distance analysis, phylogenetic reconstruction, and natural selection pressure assessment provides a suitable framework for simultaneously studying the evolutionary conservation and molecular diversity of TP53. Given the limited number of species examined, the present results should be interpreted within the context of this specific dataset, and future investigation of a greater number of species—particularly those with notable differences in lifespan, body size, and cancer susceptibility—could contribute to a more precise understanding of TP53 evolutionary patterns and their relationship to cancer control mechanisms.
